Common Tactics Used by Alienators

Recognize recurring behavioral strategies involving access, information, narrative, coercion and vulnerable-parent isolation.

Alienators tend to rely on a predictable set of behaviors that appear across many families experiencing Adult Sibling Alienation Syndrome (ASAS). While each situation is unique, the tactics below form a recognizable pattern. They are not clinical diagnoses — they are behavioral strategies used to gain control, reshape narratives, harm targeted siblings, and isolate vulnerable parents.

1. Access Control (Gatekeeping)

Restricting or controlling access to a vulnerable parent.

Examples include:

  • Claiming the parent is “too tired” or “too confused” for visits
  • Canceling or blocking communication

Creating logistical barriers (distance, timing, excuses)

  • Gatekeeping is one of the most powerful tools because proximity equals influence.

2. Information Blackouts

Withholding updates, medical information, decisions, or changes in the parent’s condition.

This tactic keeps the alienator in control of the narrative and prevents targeted siblings from participating meaningfully in care or decision‑making.

3. Narrative Rewriting

Gradually reshaping family history to justify exclusion.

This may involve:

  • Recasting childhood events
  • Minimizing the targeted sibling’s contributions
  • Exaggerating grievances

Presenting themselves as the long‑suffering hero

  • Narrative rewriting is often subtle and accumulates over time.

4. Whisper Campaigns

Quiet, indirect rumor‑spreading designed to damage the targeted sibling’s reputation.

Whisper campaigns rely on:

  • Hints
  • Suggestions
  • “Concerns”

Emotional storytelling

  • The goal is to plant doubt without making overt accusations.

5. Smear Tactics

More direct attacks on the targeted sibling’s character.

These may include:

  • Claims of irresponsibility
  • Accusations of danger or instability

Misrepresentation of past events

  • Smear tactics justify exclusion and reinforce the alienator’s control.

6. Triangulation

Using a third party — often a parent or another sibling — to relay messages, escalate conflict, or reinforce division.

Triangulation prevents direct communication and increases misunderstanding.

7. Crisis Manipulation

Creating, exaggerating, or exploiting crises to justify control.

Common during:

  • Medical emergencies
  • Care transitions

Financial decisions

  • The alienator positions themselves as the indispensable problem‑solver.

8. Caregiving Monopoly

Positioning themselves as the sole or primary caregiver.

This monopoly becomes a tool for:

  • Controlling access
  • Controlling information

Controlling decision‑making

  • The role is often used to claim moral superiority.

9. Identity Erosion

Gradually wearing down the targeted sibling’s identity through repeated mischaracterization.

Over time, the targeted sibling may be portrayed as:

  • Unreliable
  • Dangerous
  • Uncaring

Irresponsible

  • Identity erosion is a long‑term tactic that reshapes how others perceive the targeted sibling.

10. Emotional Coercion

Using guilt, fear, obligation, or emotional pressure to influence others.

Often framed as:

  • “I’m just trying to protect Mom.”
  • “You don’t understand how hard this is.”

“I’m the only one who cares.”

  • Coercion masks control as concern.

11. Boundary Violations

Ignoring or manipulating personal boundaries to maintain dominance.

Examples include:

  • Overstepping decision‑making roles
  • Interfering in private conversations

Monitoring or controlling communication

  • Boundary violations reinforce dependency and control.

12. Selective Competence

Appearing highly competent in front of outsiders while behaving divisively behind the scenes.

This dual presentation helps alienators maintain credibility and influence.

13. Manufactured Loyalty Tests

Creating situations where family members must “choose sides.”

These tests often involve:

  • Emotional ultimatums
  • False dilemmas

Pressure to agree with the alienator’s narrative

  • Loyalty tests fracture relationships and reinforce the alienator’s authority.

14. Escalation During Vulnerability

Alienators often intensify tactics during periods of:

  • Illness
  • Cognitive decline
  • Financial stress

Major life transitions

  • These moments provide opportunities to consolidate control.

15. Strategic Absence (Ghosting)

Disappearing during conflict or responsibility, then reappearing to influence outcomes.

Absence becomes a form of passive control.

16. Enlisting Henchmen / Pawns

Recruiting others to repeat narratives, apply pressure, or reinforce exclusion.

These individuals may not fully understand the situation but become part of the alienation system.

17. Over‑Documentation (Archivist Behavior)

Collecting selective “evidence” — texts, emails, notes — to support a narrative.

This documentation is often curated to present the alienator as responsible and the targeted sibling as problematic.

18. Public Virtue Signaling

Displaying exaggerated helpfulness, generosity, or moral superiority in public settings.

This creates a contrast between the alienator’s public persona and their private behavior.

How These Tactics Work Together

Alienation rarely occurs through a single tactic.

Instead, alienators build a system:

  • Control access
  • Control information
  • Control narrative
  • Control perception
  • Control decision‑making

The result is a closed loop where the targeted sibling is increasingly isolated, misrepresented, and excluded.
