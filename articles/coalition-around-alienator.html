Alienation Thrives Because of the Coalition Around the Alienator

A look at the roles and social structures that can reinforce alienation dynamics.

Here we will take a look at the structure that feeds the alienators power. This is the system that forms — often unintentionally — around the person driving the alienation. It’s not a single villain; it’s a network of roles, each contributing in different ways. Some are active participants. Others are passive failures. All shape the outcome.

1. The Architect (Primary Alienator)

The central figure.

Creates the narrative, defines the “problem,” and positions themselves as the solution.

Their power comes from confidence, proximity, emotional dominance and a need for control

2. The Passive Good Person

This is the most painful category — people who should have done the right thing but didn’t.

Their failings:

  • They avoid conflict because it’s uncomfortable.
  • They assume “everyone has their side” instead of investigating.
  • They don’t want to risk their own standing in the family or community.
  • They underestimate the seriousness of the situation.
  • They believe neutrality is kindness, when neutrality is actually complicity.

Why they matter:

Alienation rarely succeeds without them.

Their silence is the oxygen the alienator breathes.

3. The Intellectualizer

These people use logic, philosophy, religious platitudes, or “objectivity” to avoid emotional responsibility.

They typically frame the alienator’s narrative as “reasonable” because it’s easier than confronting the truth.

Failing: moral cowardice disguised as rationality.

4. The Authority Figure

Pastor, professor, community leader.

Their endorsement — even passive — gives the alienator coveted legitimacy.

Failing: confusing their positional authority with moral clarity often wanting a quick wrap up rather than involvement. Lazy and sloppy is their signature brand

5. The Fearful Loyalist

Has a long history of being intimidated by the alienator.

Supports them out of fear, not conviction.

Failing: choosing safety over integrity.

6. The Beneficiary

Stands to gain financially, socially, or emotionally.

They don’t need to lie — they just need to stay quiet.

Failing: self-interest disguised as “staying out of it.”

7. The Conflict-Avoider

Hates tension.

Will agree with whoever is loudest, closest, or most insistent.

Failing: mistaking comfort for peace.

8. The Gatekeeper (often the alienator)

Controls access to the parent or information.

Often they don't see themselves as part of the problem.

Failing: believing logistics are neutral when they’re actually power.

9. The Echo

Repeats the alienator’s narrative without verifying anything.

Often younger family members or those emotionally dependent on the alienator.

Failing: confusing loyalty with truth.

10. The Ghost

Disappears entirely.

Doesn’t ask questions, doesn’t intervene, doesn’t show up.

Failing: absence at the moment when presence mattered.
