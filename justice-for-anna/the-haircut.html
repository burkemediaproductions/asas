The Haircut

A personal reflection on identity, autonomy and a moment that came to symbolize a painful family transition.

There are moments in family estrangement that don’t arrive with shouting, paperwork, police reports or courtroom dramas. Some moments slip in quietly, disguised as “practical,” wrapped in the soft language of caregiving. But if you know the history, the vulnerabilities, the private meanings behind certain choices, you can feel the violence in the room even when no one raises their voice.

My mother’s long hair defines one of those moments.

She had worn it long for decades, thick, silver, wavy, often pulled back in a ponytail or braid that swung like a heavy rope when she danced or turned her head quickly. It was a hard earned achievement badge signifying her power to make choices. Additionally, it was a throwback joy connecting her to bygone days and old church-lady traditions. But it was also much more; it was a quiet rebellion against a childhood memory she never stopped recalling: the day her own mother promised to “just trim” her long hair and instead cut it savagely short, while laughing at her tears. Mom had cried for days. She carried the memory of that humiliation for more than seventy-five years.

Her long hair became a symbol of her recovery from that experience — a small sovereignty she protected as age and blindness slowly stole other freedoms. That ponytail was proof that she could still choose something about the shape of her own life.

The Moment Everything Shifted

When she arrived in a new state to stay with relatives, her hair was cut immediately. Not eventually. Not after settling in. Not because of any medical or hygienic need. It happened right away: so fast that one might have thought her hair was an urgent problem requiring swift intervention.

In some families, especially in cases of Adult Sibling Alienation Syndrome (ASAS), control can begin with gestures that look helpful but carry symbolic weight. A caregiver may remove something the parent loves. They may alter a visible part of their identity. They may create a “before and after” that marks the parent as having crossed into someone else’s territory. We certainly can't know what anyone meant by it, but to us the effect felt transformative in a very painful way, almost like an erasure of her identity.

My mom sat quietly in a chair while her hair, her pride, her history, her autonomy, fell to the floor in long soft silver-gray coils.

Some people will insist it was practical. Easier to manage. Cleaner. “For her own good.” But we had cared for her for years. Her long hair was never a burden. She brushed it and braided it by herself and friends were always glad to help. She always kept it neat. It was never a tangled mess, never unmanageable. It was simply hers. She enjoyed the quiet reminder that she had overcome a deep hurt, and of course she loved comparing her ponytail to the ponytails of various country boy cousins and friends. Perhaps that joy was a little part of what made it meaningful.

A Pattern Seen in Adult Sibling Alienation Syndrome

In many ASAS narratives, the first shift is a subtle one. A symbolic act. A change that seems small but carries huge emotional weight. Something that signals a new hierarchy. Something that quietly says: Your past is irrelevant now. Your preferences are irrelevant now.

Whether intended or not, these gestures can feel like a powerful psychological reset — a way of making an aging senior smaller, more dependent, more compliant. A way of marking territory, or claiming authority.

At the time, we didn’t fully understand it. We only felt the deep wrongness of it. When the photo arrived, the collective sadness was an oppressive cloud of inescapable dread. Looking back, it was the first clear sign of what was coming. The moment where we felt that the story shifted from caregiving to something darker, perhaps something orchestrated and perhaps something that would eventually take our mother away from us entirely.

Why We Allowed the Move

People ask why we let her go.

The truth is simple: we trusted; in the easy way decent people trust their family. We had never heard of Adult Sibling Alienation Syndrome. We didn’t know the pathology. We didn't understand what ASAS was and we had never heard of dark personality trait clusters or disorders that show up when parents decline. We sensed that the haircut was an omen. We felt like it wasn’t an act of care but rather a moment heavy with symbolism. We didn’t yet know what that meant.

But now we know.

And we will never forget the sadness and foreboding we felt the first time we saw the picture. In our minds I think we each saw the silver strands of her autonomy lying on the floor, I often wondered how many times our sensitive forgetful momma reflexively reached up for the assurance of that ponytail only to be reminded anew of its absence. As I envisioned her cheek streaked by silent tears I sensed the quiet beginning of an ending none of us saw coming.

This narrative reflects our personal recollections, emotional experiences, and interpretation of events, and uses recognized ASAS patterns documented in estrangement and alienation literature. It does not assert anyone’s intentions, actions, or identity as fact.
