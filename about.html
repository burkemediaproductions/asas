About the Framework

About ASAS

Adult Sibling Alienation Syndrome is an educational framework for understanding relational patterns that can fracture adult sibling relationships and place vulnerable parents at risk.

Adult Sibling Alienation Syndrome (ASAS) is an educational framework developed to help families, professionals, and researchers understand the relational patterns that lead to estrangement between adult siblings. ASAS is not a clinical diagnosis. It is a descriptive model that identifies how undue influence, narrative-shaping, and communication breakdowns can fracture sibling relationships and destabilize family systems, often putting vulnerable parents at risk.

ASAS grew out of lived experience and extensive observation of recurring patterns across many families. Studies show that between 25-37% of families experience some degree of adult sibling estrangement or alienation behaviors, often triggered by an aging and vulnerable parent’s care or estate.

While the framework is informed by personal insight, it does not describe any specific individual. Instead, it reflects dynamics seen repeatedly in adult sibling estrangement: the emergence of an alienating sibling, the isolation of a targeted sibling, and the involvement of vulnerable family members who may be drawn into the conflict or placed in harm's way.

Our work focuses on clarity, structure, and resources. Many people experiencing sibling alienation struggle to find language for what is happening. ASAS provides terminology, explanations, and guidance that help individuals understand the dynamics at play without assigning blame or making clinical claims. The goal is to illuminate patterns, reduce confusion, and encourage healthier communication within families.

ASAS is committed to presenting information responsibly, respectfully, and without accusation. We strive to help families recognize alienation dynamics, understand the impact, and move toward stability, transparency, and when possible, healthier long-term relationships.

Some sibling-alienation situations become so toxic or entrenched that healthy communication is no longer realistic or safe. In these cases, attempts at dialogue often escalate conflict, deepen distortion, or expose vulnerable family members to further harm.

ASAS acknowledges that not all relational systems can be repaired through improved communication; some patterns—chronic boundary-violations, coercive control, exploitation of a vulnerable parent, legal manipulations and courtroom battles—require a shift in priority from reconciliation to protection and outside help. The framework recognizes these realities without clinical labeling: in some families, the safest path forward is clarity, distance, and safeguarding the well-being of both the targeted sibling and the vulnerable parent.

Important distinction

Educational framework

ASAS is presented as a descriptive model, not a clinical diagnosis.
