Educational Guide for the CIEM-16 (Circle of Influence Expansion Module)

Understanding the Alienators Circle of Influence Expansion tactics in Adult Sibling Alienation

When sibling alienation escalates, it doesn’t always stay inside the family. Sometimes the alienator begins extending their influence outward — shaping how friends, neighbors, professionals, and even institutions and employers view the situation.

This guide helps families recognize those patterns early, understand why they matter, and know what steps can help protect not only the alienated sibling but also act to protect a vulnerable parent. The following is a simple non clinical guide for families.

“Signs to Watch For” Checklist

1. Social Circles & Community Influence

Some alienators begin sharing selective or misleading stories with:

family friends

neighbors

church or religious communities

employers or business partners

This can create sympathy for one person while creating hostility toward others or isolating them.

It often shows up as “I’m the only one who cares” or “They don’t understand what’s really happening.” "I only want what is best for Mom" etc.

2. Professional Influence

Professionals sometimes hear only one side of the story.

This can happen with:

doctors

attorneys

financial advisors

hospice teams

When one sibling becomes the “sole narrator,” professionals may unknowingly form opinions based on very incomplete or dishonest information.

3. Gatekeeping Information & Access

A person may begin controlling:

what information reaches others

who gets access to the parent

how outsiders perceive the situation

This can sound like:

“Mom doesn’t want visitors,” or "Mom can't travel" or “The doctor said you shouldn’t be involved.”

Often, these claims are never verified or are unverifiable.

4. Strategic Positioning

Some individuals build alliances in advance of:

estate decisions

power‑of‑attorney changes

guardianship disputes

This can create a sense of inevitability — as if their version of events is already “official.”

5. Digital Influence

Modern conflict often spreads through:

social media

group chats

curated digital narratives

These channels can amplify misinformation quickly and exclude targeted siblings completely.

6. Workplace & Professional Spillover

In severe cases, conflict spills into a sibling’s professional life:

contacting employers, business partners or clients

damaging reputation

leveraging one’s own professional status, title, or wealth, to appear more credible

This can have long‑term consequences.

Why These Patterns Matter

External influence can:

isolate a vulnerable parent or put a vulnerable parent in harm's way

distort medical or legal decisions and exploit hospice and caregiver vulnerabilities

damage or destroy sibling relationships

create long‑term financial or estate consequences

Recognizing the signs early helps families protect elderly parents by ensuring clarity, fairness, and safety.

What Families Can Do

Verify claims directly with professionals

Keep communication factual and calm

Document important interactions

Ensure the parent has multiple points of contact on a frequent level

Seek neutral third‑party support when needed

You are not alone.

These patterns are common in families under the stress of alienation behaviors, and early awareness can make a meaningful difference.

CIEM-16 Assessment

Circle of Influence Expansion Module

Domain 1 — Social Recruitment

1. Family Friends The alienator shares selective, distorted, or false narratives with family friends to gain sympathy or isolate targeted sibling(s).

2. Neighbors / Local Community The alienator recruits neighbors or community members to validate their narrative or monitor the parent or a sibling.

3. Religious / Church Community The alienator leverages religious identity or church networks to portray themselves as the protector and cast siblings as untrustworthy.

Domain 2 — Professional Manipulation

4. Medical Providers Controls access to appointments, filters information, or presents themselves as the sole reliable caregiver.

5. Legal Professionals Shapes the narrative with attorneys, portraying siblings as unstable or estranged.

6. Financial Professionals Attempts to influence financial advisors, bankers, or trust officers to gain access or control.

Domain 3 — Institutional Gatekeeping

7. Information Control Restricts or distorts information flowing to outsiders to maintain a one-sided narrative.

8. Access Control Limits or blocks siblings’ access to the vulnerable parent through social or professional intermediaries.

9. Narrative Laundering Uses third parties to repeat or legitimize their narrative, creating an echo chamber.

Domain 4 — Strategic Influence Expansion

10. Pre-Legal Positioning Builds alliances with professionals in anticipation of future disputes (POA, estate, guardianship).

11. Reputation Damage Campaign Spreads negative or false claims about siblings to harm credibility or social standing.

12. Institutional Entrenchment Establishes themselves as the “default authority” across multiple domains.

Domain 5 — Digital Manipulation & Surveillance

13. Digital Narrative Control Uses social media or group chats to spread selective narratives or exclude siblings.

14. Technological Gatekeeping / Surveillance Monitors or restricts digital access related to the parent (medical portals, email, devices).

Domain 6 — Workplace & Professional Spillover

15. Workplace Reputation Interference Contacts or influences the sibling’s employer or colleagues to damage credibility.

16. Professional Boundary Violations Uses their own professional status to legitimize their narrative or exert undue influence.

Advice to Alienated Siblings dealing with High Scores on CIEM‑16

This guidance is non‑legal and non‑clinical. In practice you will find it to be genuinely useful and highly educational. Subject matter expert Dr Susan Heitler recommends a communication strategy with alienators which she labels BIFF. Keep it Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm. DO NOT respond with anger, accusations, name calling, threats, etc. You will only arm the alienator by doing this! It WILL be used against you!

1. Document everything

High CIEM scores indicate multi‑domain influence.

Families should keep:

dates

emails

texts

appointment logs

statements from professionals

inconsistencies in narratives

Documentation protects clarity and reduces future disputes.

2. Reduce emotional engagement with the alienator

High‑expansion alienators thrive on emotional reactions.

Encourage:

calm, brief communication

written communication when possible

avoiding arguments about “the narrative”

This protects the targeted sibling’s credibility.

3. Increase direct contact with professionals

If the alienator is filtering information, targeted siblings should:

call medical offices directly

request copies of records

ask for independent explanations

attend appointments when appropriate

This helps break information bottlenecks.

4. Strengthen boundaries around the vulnerable parent

High CIEM scores often correlate with elder risk.

Families can:

schedule independent visits

verify claims made by the alienator

ensure the parent has multiple points of contact

This reduces isolation.

5. Seek neutral third‑party involvement early

Not legal advice — but a general principle:

mediators

elder‑care coordinators

family therapists

patient advocates

Neutral professionals reduce distortion and triangulation.

6. Avoid retaliatory behavior

High CIEM cases can tempt targeted siblings to “fight back.”

This almost always worsens the situation.

Encourage:

calm consistency

factual communication

avoiding counter‑narratives

Credibility is the targeted sibling’s strongest asset.

7. Focus on protecting the vulnerable parent, not “winning”

High CIEM scores indicate systemic risk.

The priority becomes:

safety of parent

access to parent

accurate information about parent

preventing isolation of parent

Not proving the alienator wrong.
